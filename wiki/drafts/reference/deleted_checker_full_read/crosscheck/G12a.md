Most of this group survives, and it survives in a stronger form. The old code's eight evaluators of `requires` facts are gone: a data `requires` is now one `DataDomain` obligation per clause, minted by stage 05 and proved by stage 06 through the kernel, and the invariant-window rules (R51 to R58) are one structural walk with tests for each case. Multi-state machines, case-level `requires`, named evidence bindings, recursive predicates, erased record arguments and continuations are not checked at all: stage 05 refuses the construct, so the rules about them cannot be broken silently.

Two findings stand out. First, the duplicate-name rule (R8) is now only partly enforced. The check that used `source_scopes_separate` to refuse two same-named declarations survives for `const` only (`02/constants/uses.rs:15-35`); I found no declaration-site refusal of two `data`, two `trait`, a `data` and a `trait`, or two parameters of one machine with the same name. Second, the public-exposes-private rule (R1) covers machine signatures, calls and product paths, but I found no check for a public `const` type, a public conformance header or a public domain alias constituent, and the surviving check says itself that a domain written as a constraint in a public signature "is not judged here yet" (`02/names/public_interfaces.rs:16-18`). The other "not found" rules (R6, R9, R71, R73, R107) concern narrow vocabulary; R96 is an acceptance shortcut whose absence makes the compiler stricter, not looser.

The stage 05 README's "subset" section understates what the code serves: domains with predicates, floats, arrays, byte views and mathematical premises all have readers (`05/read/predicates.rs`, `float_meaning.rs`, `arrays.rs`, `mathematics.rs`). I classified from the code, not from that section.
